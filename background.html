Background

Post-Bayesian methods produce a distribution over parameters or predictions, as Bayesian inference does, but use an update rule other than Bayes' rule. This page gives a short overview of the topics used to tag papers in the repository (three post-Bayesian approaches and the related PAC-Bayes theory) and points to a key paper for each. It is a starting point, not a survey: the papers themselves are the authoritative source.

Why Go Beyond Bayes?

Given a model \(p(x_{1:n}\mid\theta)\) and a prior \(\pi(\theta)\), Bayes' theorem gives the posterior

$$\pi_n(\theta\mid x_{1:n}) \;=\; \frac{p(x_{1:n}\mid\theta)\,\pi(\theta)}{\int p(x_{1:n}\mid\theta)\,\pi(\theta)\,\mathrm d\theta}.$$

Using this posterior as a summary of uncertainty about \(\theta\) is well justified when three assumptions hold (Knoblauch, Jewson & Damoulas, 2022):

  • (A1) The model is well specified: \(x_{1:n}\sim p(x_{1:n}\mid\theta^\ast)\) for some \(\theta^\ast\in\Theta\).
  • (A2) The prior is well specified: \(\pi(\theta)\) honestly encodes prior uncertainty about \(\theta\).
  • (A3) The posterior is computable in practice, exactly or to a good approximation.

In practice these assumptions are sometimes violated: models are simplifications, priors are often chosen for convenience, and the posterior may only be available approximately. This motivates considering alternatives.

A useful starting point is to view Bayes' rule as one particular update rule, which maps a prior and data to a posterior. Bissiri, Holmes & Walker (2016) frame belief updating as a decision problem: the updated distribution minimises the expected loss on the data plus the Kullback–Leibler divergence to the prior. With the negative log-likelihood as the loss this recovers Bayes' rule; with other losses it gives a coherent update for other quantities of interest. Changing the ingredients of this problem (the loss, the divergence, or what is being scored) gives other update rules, and the first three topics below are different ways of doing so.

Generalised Bayes

Generalised Bayes keeps the prior and the form of Bayes' rule, but replaces the negative log-likelihood with a loss function \(\mathsf L(x_{1:n}, p_\theta)\) chosen for the task at hand. The result is the Gibbs (generalised) posterior

$$\pi^{\mathsf L}_n(\theta\mid x_{1:n}) \;=\; \frac{\exp\{-\lambda\,\mathsf L(x_{1:n},p_\theta)\}\,\pi(\theta)}{\int \exp\{-\lambda\,\mathsf L(x_{1:n},p_\theta)\}\,\pi(\theta)\,\mathrm d\theta},$$

where \(\lambda>0\) is a learning rate. The standard posterior is the special case \(\mathsf L = -\log p(x_{1:n}\mid\theta)\), \(\lambda = 1\). For additive losses \(\mathsf L = \sum_i \ell(x_i, p_\theta)\), Bissiri, Holmes & Walker (2016) show this is the coherent way to update beliefs about the loss minimiser, giving the sequential update \(\pi^{\mathsf L}_n \propto \pi^{\mathsf L}_{n-1}\,e^{-\lambda \ell(x_n,p_\theta)}\). The loss need not come from a probabilistic model at all. Equivalently, by the Donsker–Varadhan variational formula, the Gibbs posterior is the distribution that best trades off expected loss against closeness to the prior:

$$\pi^{\mathsf L}_n \;=\; \arg\min_{q\in\mathcal P(\Theta)} \Big\{\mathbb E_{\theta\sim q}\big[\mathsf L(x_{1:n},p_\theta)\big] + \tfrac{1}{\lambda}\,\mathrm{KL}(q,\pi)\Big\}.$$

The Log-Loss with \(\lambda\neq 1\): Power Posteriors

Keeping the log-likelihood but changing \(\lambda\) gives power (fractional, tempered) posteriors, \(\pi^{(\lambda)}_n \propto p(x_{1:n}\mid\theta)^{\lambda}\,\pi(\theta)\), which trade off prior against data. Choosing \(\lambda<1\) (e.g. with Grünwald's SafeBayes) can improve finite-sample risk under misspecification, and makes the posterior amenable to PAC-Bayes analysis (see below). Coarsened posteriors (Miller & Dunson, 2019) are approximately power posteriors with \(\lambda=\alpha/(\alpha+n)\approx \alpha/n\). But any fixed \(\lambda\) is eventually washed out by the data: the predictive converges to the plug-in predictive \(p_{\hat\theta_n}\) (McLatchie, Fong, Frazier & Knoblauch, 2025). Changing \(\lambda\) alone can improve parameter uncertainty, but it neither fixes prediction under misspecification nor gives robustness. For that, the loss itself has to change.

For a general loss, \(\lambda\) still controls how fast the posterior concentrates, and therefore whether its credible sets are calibrated. Proposals for setting it include information matching (Holmes & Walker, 2017) and bootstrap calibration (Syring & Martin, 2019). Wu & Martin (2023) compare the options.

Computation

A major practical benefit of generalised Bayes is computational: the loss can be chosen so that the posterior is cheap to compute, even when the standard posterior is not.

  • Intractable likelihoods. Losses based on Stein discrepancies or score matching do not need the likelihood's normalising constant, so they apply to unnormalised and doubly intractable models (Matsubara et al., 2022; 2023).
  • Conjugacy. Weighted score-matching losses are quadratic in the natural parameter of exponential families, giving closed-form posteriors that cost no more than standard Bayes: for example robust and conjugate Gaussian processes and conjugate inference for discrete doubly intractable models.
  • Simulator-based models. Losses such as scoring rules or MMD only need samples from the model (Pacchiardi et al., 2024). Plugging simulation-based loss estimates into MCMC gives pseudo-marginal schemes that target the wrong distribution unless the number of simulations grows with \(n\); Zig-Zag samplers avoid this because they only need unbiased gradient estimates (Frazier et al., 2024).

Robust Losses

Another reason to change the loss is robustness to outliers, formalised as Huber contamination \(P_{\varepsilon,x} = (1-\varepsilon)P + \varepsilon\,\delta_x\). View the loss as a functional of the empirical measure \(P_n=\frac1n\sum_i\delta_{x_i}\), and the posterior density at each \(\theta\) as a statistical functional. The key condition for global bias-robustness is then that the loss is uniformly insensitive to outliers:

$$\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\,\sup_{x}\,\Big|\tfrac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm d\varepsilon}\,\mathsf L(P_{\varepsilon,x}, p_\theta)\big|_{\varepsilon=0}\Big| \;<\; \infty.$$

The log-likelihood violates this for almost every model, including the Gaussian. Robust losses are typically built as estimators of robust divergences \(D(q, p_\theta)\): \(\beta\)- and \(\gamma\)-divergences, MMD, and (weighted) Stein discrepancies.

Predictively Oriented Posteriors

Bayes posteriors are fragile for prediction under misspecification. As \(n\to\infty\) the posterior collapses to a point mass at \(\hat\theta_n\) (typically at rate \(\sqrt n\)), so the posterior predictive \(p_{\pi_n}=\int p_\theta\,\mathrm d\pi_n(\theta)\) collapses onto the plug-in predictive \(p_{\hat\theta_n}\). This is sensible if the model is correct, since no uncertainty is left once all the data have been seen. If the model is wrong it makes little sense, because some uncertainty is irreducible. Power and Gibbs posteriors behave the same way.

The key observation is that all Gibbs posteriors, including Bayes, minimise the averaged score of the model. Predictively oriented (PrO) posteriors (McLatchie, Chérief-Abdellatif, Frazier & Knoblauch, 2025) instead minimise the score of the averaged model. For a scoring rule \(S\) and predictive \(P_Q=\int P_\theta\,\mathrm dQ(\theta)\):

$$\text{Gibbs:}\quad Q^\dagger_n = \arg\min_{Q\in\mathcal P(\Theta)}\Big\{\frac{\lambda_n}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \int S(P_\theta,x_i)\,\mathrm dQ(\theta) + \mathrm{KL}(Q,\Pi)\Big\},$$ $$\text{PrO:}\quad Q_n = \arg\min_{Q\in\mathcal P(\Theta)}\Big\{\frac{\lambda_n}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n S(P_Q,x_i) + \mathrm{KL}(Q,\Pi)\Big\}.$$

With the log score \(S(P,x)=-\log p(x)\) and \(\lambda_n=n\), the left-hand problem is exactly Bayes, while the right-hand problem scores the mixture \(-\log\int p_\theta(x_i)\,\mathrm dQ(\theta)\). Other proper scoring rules, such as the kernel score (MMD), work too. Consequences:

  • Adaptivity. If the model can recover the data-generating process, PrO posteriors concentrate like Bayes and Gibbs posteriors. Under non-trivial misspecification they do not collapse in parameter space; they collapse in predictive space, towards the mixture over the model class closest to the truth.
  • Predictive dominance. Up to logarithmic factors they converge to the predictively optimal model average \(Q^\ast=\arg\min_Q \mathbb E_{P_0}[S(P_Q,X)]\), strictly dominating Bayes and Gibbs predictives under non-trivial misspecification and matching them otherwise.
  • Interpretable uncertainty. The irreducible spread of \(Q_n\) measures the degree of model misspecification: parameter uncertainty becomes a function of predictive performance.

Computation. The objective is non-linear in \(Q\), so standard MCMC does not apply. Instead it is minimised by a Wasserstein gradient flow, implemented as an interacting particle system (mean-field Langevin dynamics):

$$\mathrm d\theta_j = -\Big(\nabla V(\theta_j) + \frac{\lambda_1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \nabla_1\kappa(\theta_j,\theta_i)\Big)\mathrm dt + \sqrt{2\lambda_2}\,\mathrm dB_j.$$

This is the form taken for the kernel (MMD) score, whose objective is quadratic in \(Q\): \(\int V\,\mathrm dQ+\tfrac{\lambda_1}{2}\iint\kappa\,\mathrm dQ\,\mathrm dQ+\lambda_2\int q\log q\). Here \(N\) particles \(\theta_1,\dots,\theta_N\) approximate \(Q_n\), the potential \(V\) collects the data term and \(-\log\pi\), the pairwise interaction \(\kappa(\theta,\theta')\propto\iint k\,\mathrm dP_\theta\,\mathrm dP_{\theta'}\) comes from scoring the mixture, and \(B_j\) are independent Brownian motions. Other scores, such as the log score, lead to more general mean-field drifts.

The PAC-Bayesian precursors PAC\(^2\)-Bayes (Masegosa, 2020) and PAC\(^m\)-Bayes (Morningstar et al., 2022) target similar objectives for Bayesian deep learning, but do not define or study the PrO posterior as a general inferential method.

Martingale Posteriors

Sometimes it is easier to reason about uncertainty directly on the data. Fong, Holmes & Walker (2023) treat the missing observations as the source of uncertainty: given the whole population, the quantity of interest would be known exactly. Inference proceeds by forward simulation, or predictive resampling:

$$X_{n+i}\sim p_{n+i-1}\big(\cdot\mid x_{1:n}, X_{n+1:n+i-1}\big),\qquad i=1,2,\dots$$ $$\theta_\infty = \arg\min_{\theta\in\Theta}\int \ell(z,\theta)\,\mathrm dF_\infty(z),\qquad F_\infty=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac1N\Big(\sum_{i=1}^n\delta_{x_i}+\sum_{i=n+1}^{N}\delta_{X_i}\Big).$$

The distribution of \(\theta_\infty\) is the martingale posterior. There are two “dials”:

  • Dial 1: the sequence of one-step-ahead predictives \(\{p_n(z\mid x_{1:n})\}\) models data uncertainty, in place of a prior over parameters.
  • Dial 2: a general loss \(\ell\) summarises the information in the (imputed) population, in place of a likelihood.

For \(\theta_\infty\) to be well defined the predictives \(p_N\) (equivalently, the empirical distribution of the completed sequence) must converge almost surely. A martingale condition on the predictives guarantees this, via the martingale convergence theorem:

$$\mathbb E_{X_{n+1}\sim p_n(\cdot\mid x_{1:n})}\big[\,p_{n+1}(z\mid x_{1:n}, X_{n+1})\,\big] \;=\; p_n(z\mid x_{1:n}).$$

Choosing the Bayesian posterior predictive \(p(x_{n+1}\mid x_{1:n})=\int p(x_{n+1}\mid\theta)\,\pi_n(\theta\mid x_{1:n})\,\mathrm d\theta\) and taking \(\theta_\infty\) to be the limit of the posterior means recovers exactly the Bayes posterior, \(\theta_\infty\sim\pi_n(\theta\mid x_{1:n})\), by Doob's theorem. The empirical (Pólya-urn) predictive gives the Bayesian bootstrap. Other predictives, such as copula-based recursive updates, give fast nonparametric inference without ever writing down a prior. An active direction is to use modern predictive models such as foundation models, diffusion models or TabPFN-style networks as the predictive.

PAC-Bayes: A Related Framework

PAC-Bayes is not itself a post-Bayesian method, but it is closely connected to them. It provides generalisation bounds that hold simultaneously for every distribution \(q\) over parameters. The bounds follow from a change-of-measure inequality together with a concentration inequality, and do not assume that \(q\) was obtained by Bayes' rule. Their value here is that they can be used to analyse post-Bayesian posteriors and to design new ones. With data \(X_i\sim P_0\), empirical risk \(R_n(\theta)=\frac1n\sum_i\ell(x_i,\theta)\) and population risk \(R(\theta)=\mathbb E_{P_0}[\ell(X,\theta)]\), a basic bound reads as follows. If the “Hoeffding assumption” \(\mathbb E_{P_0}\!\int e^{\lambda[R(\theta)-R_n(\theta)]}\,\mathrm d\pi(\theta)\le e^{\lambda^2C/n}\) holds, then with probability at least \(1-\delta\), simultaneously for all \(q\in\mathcal P(\Theta)\),

$$\int R\,\mathrm dq \;\le\; \int R_n\,\mathrm dq + \frac{\lambda C}{n} + \frac{\mathrm{KL}(q,\pi) + \log(1/\delta)}{\lambda}.$$

The right-hand side is minimised by the Gibbs posterior \(q\propto \pi\,e^{-\lambda R_n}\). Here \(\lambda\) multiplies the average risk, so in the notation of the Gibbs posterior above the learning rate is \(\lambda/n\) (with the log loss, \(\lambda=n\) recovers Bayes). Choosing \(\lambda\asymp\sqrt n\) makes the gap between population and empirical risk \(O(1/\sqrt n)\) for any fixed \(q\) with \(\mathrm{KL}(q,\pi)=O(1)\). In this view the prior acts as a reference measure, or regulariser, for the bound.

The Hoeffding assumption fails for \(-\log p_\theta\) in most interesting models, Gaussians included, so the basic bound says nothing about standard Bayes. Power posteriors fix this. For \(0<\lambda<1\), Alquier & Ridgway (2020) bound the expected Rényi divergence \(D_\lambda(p_\theta, p_0)\) under \(\pi^{(\lambda)}_n\) by \(\inf_q\big\{\tfrac{\lambda}{1-\lambda}\int \mathrm{KL}(p_0,p_\theta)\,\mathrm dq + \tfrac{\mathrm{KL}(q,\pi)}{n(1-\lambda)}\big\}\), which concentrates under very mild conditions, including settings where the \(\lambda=1\) posterior does not. PAC-Bayes arguments also give posterior concentration rates for Gibbs posteriors via a prior-mass condition, complementing Bernstein–von Mises results (Miller, 2021). A good entry point is Alquier's User-friendly introduction to PAC-Bayes bounds.
